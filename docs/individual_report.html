Introduction

In our group project, my responsibility focuses on mental health need in the United States and the availability of mental health workers. I used two major public datasets: the 2024 ACS 1-Year PUMS from the Census Bureau and the 2024 BRFSS raw data from the CDC.

Initially, my idea was to compare data from 2002 with 2023. However, after reading the questionnaires, I decided that 2024 was a better choice. The 2002 questionnaires had different modules and some questions were connected to events such as September 11 attacks and depression. The 2023 questionnaires did not include the September 11 attack and depression, but includes COVID and depression. Because the question wordings and variables were different, comparing 2002 directly with 2023 could be misleading. Therefore, the superlative choice is 2024.

I have recommended to our group the 2024 survey. The goal was to understand two things: how many people report depression, and how many mental health workers are available in the United States in both census data and mobile app industry.

Data Sources

ACS 2024 1-Year PUMS

The ACS data came from the 2024 American Community Survey 1-Year Public Use Microdata Sample. This is survey data, not a full count of every person in the United States. Each person in the sample has a weight called PWGTP, which allows us to estimate the larger U.S. population.

For the ACS analysis, I used:

  • OCCP: occupation code
  • PWGTP: person weight

I used 2018 Census occupation codes to identify mental health-related workers, including counselors, social workers, psychologists, marriage and family therapists, and related occupations.

An interesting observation is that school counselors are classified under community and social service occupations, while psychiatrists and psychologists are classified under science occupations.

The ACS estimate showed approximately 3,941,701 mental health-related workers in the United States. This is not the number of respondents interviewed. It is a weighted estimate using survey weights.

BRFSS 2024

The BRFSS data came from the CDC 2024 raw file LLCP2024.XPT. BRFSS is also survey data. The 2024 file contains approximately 457,670 adult respondents.

The main depression variable used in this project was:

  • ADDEPEV3: Ever told you had a depressive disorder

In essence, this variable measures whether a person was clinically diagnosed with depression by a health professional.

I also used:

  • MENTHLTH: number of days mental health was not good
  • _LLCPWT: survey weight
  • _METSTAT: metro or non-metro status
  • SEXVAR: sex
  • _AGE80: age, top-coded at 80

Main Variables

The main target variable in the BRFSS analysis was ADDEPEV3, which identifies whether a respondent was ever told by a health professional that they had a depressive disorder.

The explanatory or stratification variables were:

  • metro status
  • sex
  • age group

I created derived variables such as depression_status, metro_status, and age_group to make the tables and charts easier to interpret.

Important Note About Weights

A major part of this project is understanding the difference between raw respondents and weighted totals.

For BRFSS, the raw dataset has approximately 457,670 respondents. However, when I use _LLCPWT, the results represent the estimated U.S. adult population.

For ACS, the same idea applies. The PUMS file is a sample, but PWGTP expands the sample to estimate the full population.

Therefore, the number 3,941,701 mental health workers is not the number of people interviewed. It is a weighted estimate of mental health workers in the United States.

This distinction between respondents and weighted totals is extremely important because misunderstanding survey weights can lead to incorrect conclusions.

Depression in Metro and Non-Metro Areas

One important finding from the BRFSS analysis is that more people who reported a depression diagnosis live in cities. Using weighted totals, the number of people who said “Yes” to depression diagnosis was much larger in metro areas than in non-metro areas.

My groupmate Helly used proportion estimates instead of total weighted counts, and the difference between metro and non-metro areas became much smaller. Soon after, we both agreed on our findings.

This created an important distinction:

  • Weighted totals show the total burden.
  • Proportions show the relative share within each group.

This was one of the most important findings in the project because it demonstrates how interpretation changes depending on whether weighted totals or proportions are used.

Capacity Estimate

I also considered a hypothetical capacity calculation using Betterhelp’s subscription data.

If BetterHelp has 6,000 providers and each provider works 15 hours per day:

  • 6,000 × 15 = 90,000 provider hours per day

Over 30 days:

  • 90,000 × 30 = 2,700,000 provider hours per month

If each patient requires approximately 10 hours per month:

  • 2,700,000 ÷ 10 = 270,000 patients per month

This means that even with thousands of providers, the number of people who can be served monthly remains limited compared to the large number of adults reporting depression.

# Data Acquisition for 2024 BRFSS Mental Health Data

library(tidyverse)
library(glue)
library(httr2)

load_brfss_mental_health_data <- function(){
    
    base_dir <- file.path("data", "mentalhealth")
    
    if(!dir.exists(base_dir)){
        dir.create(base_dir,
                   showWarnings = FALSE,
                   recursive = TRUE)
    }
    
    zip_file <- file.path(base_dir, "LLCP2024XPT.zip")
    
    file_name_out <- file.path(base_dir,
                               "brfss_2024_mental_health.csv")
    
    if(!file.exists(file_name_out)){
        
        # Download ZIP if needed
        if(!file.exists(zip_file)){
            request("https://www.cdc.gov") |>
                req_url_path("brfss",
                             "annual_data",
                             "2024",
                             "files",
                             "LLCP2024XPT.zip") |>
                req_headers(`User-Agent` = "Mozilla/5.0") |>
                req_perform(zip_file)
        }
        
        
        unzip(zip_file, exdir = base_dir)
        
        
        xpt_file <- list.files(base_dir,
                               pattern = "\\.XPT$",
                               full.names = TRUE,
                               recursive = TRUE)[1]
        
       
        brfss_data <- read_xpt(xpt_file) |>
            select(
                MENTHLTH,
                ADDEPEV3,
                SEXVAR,
                `_AGE80`,
                `_LLCPWT`
            ) |>
            mutate(
                mental_health_days = case_when(
                    MENTHLTH >= 0 & MENTHLTH <= 30 ~ MENTHLTH,
                    MENTHLTH == 88 ~ 0,
                    TRUE ~ NA_real_
                ),
                depression_status = case_match(
                    ADDEPEV3,
                    1 ~ "Yes",
                    2 ~ "No",
                    7 ~ "Don't know",
                    9 ~ "Refused"
                ),
                sex = case_match(
                    SEXVAR,
                    1 ~ "Male",
                    2 ~ "Female"
                ),
                age = as.numeric(`_AGE80`),
                survey_weight = as.numeric(`_LLCPWT`)
            ) |>
            select(
                mental_health_days,
                depression_status,
                sex,
                age,
                survey_weight
            )
        
        write_csv(brfss_data, file_name_out)
    }
    
    read_csv(file_name_out, show_col_types = FALSE)
}

            mental_health_days >= 1 & mental_health_days <= 30 ~ "1 - 30",

        )
    ) |>

        weighted_total = sum(survey_weight, na.rm = TRUE),
        .groups = "drop"
    ) |>
    mutate(

        weighted_percentage = weighted_total / sum(weighted_total)
    ) |>

# Metro and non-Metro Classification of people with Depression Diagnosis. This is Proportion. 
library(tidyverse)
library(haven)
library(gt)

brfss_mental_health_2024_geo <- read_xpt(
    file.path("data",
              "mentalhealth",
              "LLCP2024.XPT")
) |>
    select(
        MENTHLTH,
        ADDEPEV3,
        SEXVAR,
        `_AGE80`,
        `_LLCPWT`,
        `_STATE`,
        `_METSTAT`
    ) |>
    mutate(
        mental_health_days = case_when(
            MENTHLTH >= 0 & MENTHLTH <= 30 ~ MENTHLTH,
            MENTHLTH == 88 ~ 0,
            TRUE ~ NA_real_
        ),
        depression_status = case_match(
            ADDEPEV3,
            1 ~ "Yes",
            2 ~ "No",
            7 ~ "Don't know",
            9 ~ "Refused"
        ),
        sex = case_match(
            SEXVAR,
            1 ~ "Male",
            2 ~ "Female"
        ),
        age = as.numeric(`_AGE80`),
        survey_weight = as.numeric(`_LLCPWT`),
        metro_status = case_match(
            `_METSTAT`,
            1 ~ "Metropolitan",
            2 ~ "Non-Metropolitan",
            .default = "Missing"
        )
    )

metro_plot_data <- brfss_mental_health_2024_geo |>
    filter(
        depression_status %in% c("Yes", "No"),
        metro_status != "Missing"
    ) |>
    group_by(metro_status, depression_status) |>
    summarize(
        weighted_total = sum(survey_weight, na.rm = TRUE),
        .groups = "drop"
    ) |>
    group_by(metro_status) |>
    mutate(
        proportion = weighted_total / sum(weighted_total)
    )

ggplot(
    metro_plot_data,
    aes(x = metro_status,
        y = proportion,
        fill = depression_status)
) +
    geom_col(position = "fill") +
    scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::percent) +
    labs(
        title = "Depression Status by Metro vs Non-Metro",
        x = "Metro Status",
        y = "Proportion",
        fill = "Depression Status"
    ) +
    theme_bw()
